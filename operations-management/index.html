The big idea

Operations management is how organizations transform inputs into outputs efficiently to meet customer needs — and this course is about matching supply with demand on purpose, not by luck.

The journey runs from the first question — what does the customer value? — through measuring processes, controlling quality, managing inventory, forecasting demand, running projects, and finally optimizing decisions with models. Each episode adds one tool; the exams ask you to use them together.

Core concepts

Explain the role of operations management and key process concepts — inventory, flow rate, and flow time, including Little's Law.

Process analysis

Analyze and evaluate processes with flow diagrams, capacity analysis, and bottleneck identification to measure and improve efficiency.

Quality

Inventory & forecasting

Optimization

Where it gets tricky — and why that's normal

Little's Law units

Flow rate per hour times flow time in hours gives inventory. Mix minutes and hours and a correct formula gives a wrong answer.

Improving the wrong step

Speeding up a non-bottleneck feels productive and changes output by exactly zero. Find the bottleneck first, every time.

EOQ is a trade-off, not a magic number

It balances ordering against holding cost under steady-demand assumptions. Real demand is lumpier — use it as a disciplined starting point.

Forecasts lag trends

Moving averages trail a rising trend by construction. If actuals keep beating your forecast, your method — not your luck — needs revisiting.

Optimization without feasibility

A Solver answer that breaks a constraint is not an answer. Check feasibility by hand before reporting any optimum.

Playing fair: AI & academic honesty

This course draws a clear line. AI is welcome as learning support: explanations, study help, additional practice examples, and general brainstorming. AI is not allowed on Knowledge Checks, Learning Practices, and Exams — no generating, solving, checking, revising, or validating answers, and no uploading assessment questions or screenshots. For collaborative group studies, meaningful AI use is required, with an AI Use Disclosure, and all AI output must be verified and corrected. Submitted work must be your own unless collaboration is explicitly allowed; when an AI use feels borderline, ask first.

Dr. K's advice from Module 1, kept verbatim in spirit: students who use study groups and AI to understand concepts usually do very well; students who use them simply to obtain answers often struggle on the proctored exams, where outside resources are not allowed.
